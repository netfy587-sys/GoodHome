A closer look at meal timing

Four Meals a Day

Not three, not six — four. Breakfast, lunch, an afternoon meal, and dinner, spaced through the day on purpose. Here's what that rhythm actually does, and whether it's worth switching to.

Photo: S'well / Unsplash

Before the schedule

How many times you eat in a day changes more than your hunger. It shapes your energy, your portion sizes, and how easy the whole day feels to manage.

The four-meal pattern splits the day into breakfast, lunch, a proper afternoon meal — not a stray biscuit, an actual small plate — and dinner. It sits between the classic three-meal structure and the tighter five-or-six-meal grazing plans some athletes and dieters use.

Meal one · Morning
Breakfast

The meal that sets the pace

Whatever you eat first tends to decide how the next few hours feel. A breakfast with real protein and some fat — eggs, yoghurt, nut butter — keeps hunger flat until lunch. A breakfast of only sugar and refined carbs does the opposite: a spike, then a crash by mid-morning.

It doesn't need to be large. It needs to be deliberate — something eaten sitting down, not grabbed on the way out the door.

Photo: Zoshua Colah / Unsplash
Meal two · Midday
Lunch

The main event, not an afterthought

In the four-meal structure, lunch carries the most weight — the biggest plate of the day, eaten when your body is most active and best able to use the energy. Rice or roti, dal, vegetables, some protein: a full, unhurried plate rather than a rushed desk sandwich.

Eating your largest meal here, rather than at night, is one of the simplest changes this pattern makes.

Photo: Alex Bayev / Unsplash
Meal three · Afternoon
The afternoon meal

The one people usually skip — and shouldn't

This is what separates a four-meal day from a three-meal one. A small, real plate around 4 or 5pm — fruit and nuts, roasted chana, a boiled egg, a bowl of yoghurt — closes the long gap between lunch and dinner that otherwise ends in vending-machine chips or a second helping at dinner out of pure hunger.

It works because it's planned. An unplanned snack is usually whatever's closest; a planned one is usually better food.

Photo: National Cancer Institute / Unsplash
Meal four · Evening
Dinner

Lighter, earlier, calmer

Because the afternoon meal already took the edge off hunger, dinner in this pattern is usually smaller and earlier than it would otherwise be — closer to sunset than to bedtime. A lighter dinner tends to mean better sleep and less of the heavy, sluggish feeling that follows a big late meal.

It's also, often, the one meal of the day eaten with other people — which matters for reasons that have nothing to do with nutrition.

Put it on a clock

What a four-meal day can look like

Not a strict prescription — a starting shape to adjust around your own routine.

7:30 AM

Breakfast

Eggs or paneer, a piece of fruit, and tea or coffee. Enough protein to hold steady until early afternoon.

1:00 PM

Lunch

The largest plate of the day — grains, dal or a protein, and at least one vegetable side, eaten away from the desk if possible.

4:30 PM

Afternoon meal

Fruit, roasted nuts or chana, and tea. Small, but eaten sitting down, not standing over a desk.

7:30 PM

Dinner

Lighter than lunch — soup, a small grain portion, and vegetables — finished at least two hours before bed.

The honest balance sheet

What it helps with, and what to watch

Steadier blood sugar

Shorter gaps between meals mean fewer sharp dips and spikes — useful for energy and for concentration through the afternoon.

Smaller, calmer dinners

Because the afternoon meal takes the edge off, dinner stops being the meal you attack out of pure hunger.

Less impulsive snacking

A planned afternoon plate crowds out the unplanned trip to the vending machine or the biscuit tin.

The fourth meal has to be real food

If the afternoon meal turns into a packet of chips, the whole benefit disappears — it becomes a fourth chance to eat poorly, not a better-timed one.

Total intake still matters

Four meals isn't a licence to eat more overall. Spread the same daily amount across four sittings, don't add a full extra meal's worth of food.

It needs a bit more planning

An extra meal means an extra thing to pack or prepare — the pattern quietly fails for people who can't plan for it.

Not universal

Who this pattern actually suits

Probably a good fit

People with a long stretch — six hours or more — between lunch and dinner.
Anyone who currently arrives at dinner overly hungry and overeats as a result.
People trying to manage energy dips in the late afternoon.
Households that can prepare or pack one extra small meal without much extra effort.

Worth reconsidering

People who genuinely aren't hungry more than three times a day — forcing a fourth meal helps no one.
Very tight or unpredictable schedules where a fourth sitting just won't happen consistently.
Anyone managing a medical condition where meal timing is already prescribed by a doctor.
People prone to treating "extra meal" as licence for extra snacking, not a replacement for it.

Where this leaves us

A rhythm, not a rule

Four meals a day isn't a diet — it's a spacing decision. For most people with a long afternoon stretch, it trades one large, hurried dinner for a calmer day with fewer hunger spikes. It only works, though, if that fourth meal stays real food eaten on purpose, not an extra excuse to snack.
